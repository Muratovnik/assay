# Search and coverage

Use when choosing search scope, investigating omissions or assessing a landscape.

## Derive the questions

Choose answer families from the user's task. A comparison needs viable options,
shared criteria and disqualifying constraints; an explanation needs observations,
plausible mechanisms and discriminating evidence; a landscape needs inclusion
boundaries and different terminology or communities. A history needs dated
events, decision authority and changes. Do not turn one domain's lifecycle into
a universal checklist.

For a material new method, inspect suitable adjacent methods or topic outlines
for overlooked questions. Keep only questions that can affect the user's task.
An analogy suggests where to look; it does not prove applicability or authorize
importing the analogue's features. Search beyond complaint wording when the
request concerns the whole workflow. A narrow caption repair can reuse evidence.

## Test the search strategy

Start with a small set of distinct concepts and suitable source types. Vary
synonyms, formal names, abbreviations and languages when relevant. Check whether
mandatory generic terms, date/language/domain filters or search syntax exclude
needed material. Use operators only where the search system supports them.

Where known relevant materials exist, use a diverse subset as a diagnostic probe.
For a missed item distinguish query exclusion, different terminology, absent
indexing and legitimate scope exclusion. Adapt the relevant query or discovery
route. Do not tune only to exact known titles, assume the probe set is complete,
or label its recall as recall over the internet.

Follow references, citations and repository trees when a likely source routes
detail elsewhere. For a skill, inspect its applicable references, commands,
examples, validators and hooks. Read only what resolves a material question;
a package overview cannot support a conclusion about every branch.

## Record the useful boundary

Keep a compact map: question -> examined location/condition -> finding or gap ->
next useful action. Distinguish sufficient evidence, missing guidance in the
examined scope, generic guidance with a weak check, inapplicability, inaccessible
and unexamined material. Record meaningful exclusions and actual queries/dates
when repeatability matters. Do not use estimated search-engine hit totals as
screened counts.

Name a gap before expanding a search. Repeated results may expose a vocabulary
or source-family trap rather than saturation. Use existing indexes, cached
versions or focused retrieval before broad crawling. New remarks revise affected
questions and conclusions; they do not automatically restart the whole survey.
